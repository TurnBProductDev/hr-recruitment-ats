The old working day

Before

Recruitment ran on a shared careers mailbox and a single master spreadsheet. Everything that moved a candidate forward — reading the CV, deciding on it, booking the interview, telling anyone about it — passed through a person retyping something that already existed somewhere else. These were the eight recurring frictions.

  • Opening the inbox and typing it all in againEvery CV that landed in the careers mailbox was opened, read, and its name, phone, email, qualification and experience keyed into the spreadsheet by hand. The attachment was then filed somewhere separate.
  • No way to know who had applied beforeA repeat applicant, or someone previously rejected for cause, looked identical to a first-time applicant. Catching it depended on somebody remembering a name.
  • Screening by reading, with nothing written downEvery CV was read against the JD from scratch. The reasoning behind a shortlist decision lived in the recruiter's head, so it could not be reviewed, compared between roles, or defended later.
  • A spreadsheet with no memoryStatus was a cell that got overwritten. Who moved a candidate, when, and why was simply gone — and two people editing the file at once meant one of them lost their work.
  • Email ping-pong to book one interviewAsk the interviewer for availability, wait, offer times to the candidate, wait, create the calendar invite, paste in a meeting link, then repeat the whole loop when one side moved.
  • Interviewers with no window into the processCVs were forwarded as email attachments, feedback came back as prose in a reply, and it was HR's job to transcribe the verdict into the sheet.
  • Writing the same emails over and overInvites, reschedules and rejections were retyped or copy-pasted from an older mail, with the wording drifting a little further from the approved version every time.
  • Month-end reporting rebuilt by handFunnel conversion, time to hire and source effectiveness meant filtering, pivoting and re-counting the spreadsheet — a day's work, out of date the moment it was circulated.

What replaced it, stage by stage

The pipeline

The numbering is the candidate's actual route through the system. Each stage is a real status the application records and reports on, and each carries what the database shows for the first 81 days of live running.

1

Applications arrive

STATUS → OPEN  ·  SOURCES: LINKEDIN, CAREERS PORTAL, AGENCIES, REFERRALS

Before

Open each mail, read the attachment, type the applicant into the spreadsheet, save the CV to a folder, hope the file name matches the row.

Now

A CV mailed to the careers address is read by the system and written straight into the candidate database — name, contact, education, skills and a written profile summary — with the file archived and linked on the record. Nobody opens the mailbox to make that happen.

499 candidates in 81 days — 167 in July, 256 in August. 100% have their CV on file; 86% arrived with an AI-written profile already attached. LinkedIn supplied 297, the careers portal 193.

2

Filing, de-duplication and blacklist check

EMAIL REGISTRY  ·  BLACKLIST  ·  GENERAL APPLICATIONS  ·  FUTURE PROSPECTS

Before

Duplicate and repeat applicants went unnoticed unless someone recognised the name. Speculative CVs with no matching vacancy piled up unsorted.

Now

Every applicant email is checked against the registry as the record is created — a reapply is flagged automatically, a blacklisted address is blocked. A CV matching no open vacancy is filed under General Applications against the role the candidate actually asked for, so the pool stays searchable instead of just accumulating.

173 candidates flagged as repeat applicants. 191 email addresses have applied more than once; the busiest applied 12 times. 96 speculative CVs are filed and searchable under General Applications rather than sitting in an inbox.

3

CV screening

STATUS → QUALIFIED / REJECTED / HOLD  ·  MATCH SCORE 0–100

Before

Read every CV against the JD, form a view, move a cell. No score, no written reason, no consistency between two recruiters or between Monday and Friday.

Now

Score Candidates runs a whole vacancy's applicants against its job description and returns one score per candidate with a written rationale — judging whether the person could actually do the job, not how many keywords their CV contains. HR reads the score and the reasoning, then decides. The scoring criteria and the wording given to the AI are both editable by an administrator, without a code change.

274 candidates scored against a JD, and the spread is doing real work: 82 scored under 40, only 20 scored above 75. Median time from application to a qualified decision is 4 days.

4

Tele screening

COMMUNICATION LOG  ·  OUTCOME RECORDED PER CALL

Before

Each recruiter invented their own call questions from the CV in front of them, and the call outcome was a note in a cell — if it was recorded at all.

Now

Every call and its outcome is logged on the candidate's timeline and feeds the dashboard's call-stage counts directly. Ten screening questions can be generated from the candidate's own CV and saved to the record, so they stay available to whoever interviews them later.

79 calls logged against candidate records, plus 148 notes on timelines — a written history where previously there was none. Generated question sets have been used on only 4 candidates so far; see the adoption gap below.

5

Interview rounds

STATUS → ROUND 1  →  ROUND 2  ·  TECHNICAL / MANAGERIAL / HR / FINAL

Before

Emails back and forth to find a slot, a calendar invite made by hand, a CV forwarded as an attachment, and feedback typed up from a reply.

Now

Interviews are scheduled against the candidate's record and the invitation goes out as a real calendar meeting the candidate can accept. Reschedules and cancellations are tracked rather than lost in a mail thread, and the interviewer enters feedback, score and the pass/fail decision directly in their own portal — no transcription by HR.

22 interviews across 6 interviewers, with 8 reschedules handled on the record. 14 of the 17 user accounts are interviewers. Results so far: 3 pass, 8 fail, 11 still awaiting a verdict.

6

Decision and close-out

STATUS → FINAL SELECTION  →  HIRED  ·  REJECTED  ·  BLACKLISTED

Before

Rejection mails were retyped one at a time, wording drifting with each copy, and candidates under a filled vacancy were closed off individually or forgotten entirely.

Now

The rejection mail arrives pre-written in the approved wording, addressed and copied to the right people, for HR to review and send. Candidates can be closed in bulk, one action rejects everyone still open under a filled vacancy, and a mis-click can be reverted from the candidate's own timeline.

160 rejections issued in consistent, approved wording. Median time from application to a rejection decision is 13 days — candidates get an answer instead of silence. One candidate has reached Final Selection; no hire has been recorded in the system yet.

Running across every stage

Always on

Four things now happen continuously, which previously either happened at month-end or did not happen at all.

  • A permanent audit trail — 924 entries and countingEvery status change is stored with who made it, when, and any remark, and it drives the Stage Dates tracker showing exactly how long each candidate sat at each step. 499 of those entries are record creations; the other 425 are decisions someone made and the system remembered. Nothing is overwritten.
  • Reporting that is already builtApplicants → Qualified → Shortlisted → Round 1 cleared → Round 2 cleared → Hired, with conversion at each step, broken down by role and source, filtered by date. Today that reads: 116 ever qualified, 39 reached Round 1, 11 reached Round 2. The Daily View shows what the desk covered on any given day, and every bar drills through to the records behind it.
  • Access by role, not by forwarding17 accounts across HR Admin, Recruiter, Interviewer and Hiring Manager, each seeing only what their role allows, signing in with their existing Microsoft work account. The 14 interviewers get their own portal instead of an inbox full of forwarded CVs.
  • Exports where they are still neededAny candidate list — filtered however it is on screen — downloads to Excel in one click. The spreadsheet is now an output for whoever wants one, not the system of record.

Built, not yet switched on

Adoption gap

Five capabilities are finished and working but barely used in production. They are listed here because the honest version of this document is more useful than the flattering one — and because each is a decision someone can take this month, not a development cost.

Interviewer proposes their own slots 0 times The availability email loop is still being run by hand. This is the single biggest remaining manual cost per interview.
Automatic Teams link on the invite 0 of 22 Needs the Microsoft Graph credentials set in the live environment. Until then, meeting links are pasted in by hand.

Two activities account for two-thirds of the saving. Logging applications and checking for repeat applicants was pure transcription — at 188 applications a month it consumed over thirty hours, and it is now essentially gone. Screening has not disappeared but has changed shape: from reading 188 CVs cold to reviewing a score and a written rationale, then deciding. Interview scheduling saves the least so far, simply because only eight interviews a month are being booked — and because the feature that would automate most of it has not been switched on.

What the system deliberately leaves to people

Boundaries

Automation stops at judgement. Every point below is a design decision, not a missing feature — the system prepares the work and a person makes the call. The audit trail shows this is real: a named person is recorded against all 924 recorded actions.

No candidate is rejected by a score. The score and its rationale are an input to a screening decision. Status only changes when someone changes it.
No email sends itself to a candidate. Invitations and rejections arrive as drafts in approved wording; the recruiter reads, edits if needed, and sends. Recipients and CC are fixed by policy rather than typed each time.
Interview verdicts are the interviewer's. Feedback, score and pass/fail are entered by the person who ran the round.
Offers, negotiation and the hire decision stay off-system. The application records the outcome; it does not make it.
The wording is yours to change. Email templates and the instructions given to the AI are both editable by an administrator, so tone and criteria stay under HR's control rather than a developer's.

TurnB ATS — applicant tracking for TurnB Business Services Pvt. Ltd. Django and Bootstrap 5, running on Azure App Service against Azure SQL, with CV and JD reading, match scoring and screening questions on Azure OpenAI, mail and document filing through Azure Logic Apps, interview calendars through Microsoft Graph, and staff sign-in through Microsoft Entra ID.

How to read these numbers. Every count on this page was queried from the live database on 15 September 2026 and covers 25 June to 14 September 2026 — 81 days, of which July and August are the only complete months. Monthly rates are the period total divided by 2.66 months. The volumes in the effort model are measured; the per-task manual times are estimates and are the part worth arguing with. No hire has yet been recorded in the system, so this document makes no claim about time to hire, cost per hire, or quality of hire.
